# Prompt Kit: The $40 Question - Three Tools for Raising Your Sights Past the Commoditized List

The thing that is scarce is not the model. It is the size of your list of things you know how to ask for. AI made execution cheap, and when execution gets cheap the value does not vanish - it moves to the questions that decide what is worth executing. A cheap model ties a $9 model on "implement this feature" not because the models are the same but because that task is one everyone already knows how to ask for, and that is exactly where models converge. The differentiator was never the price. It is your imagination, and imagination is a human task that a cheap model will never do for you.

This kit gives you three tools for finding and spending that imagination on purpose:

1. **The Task-List Diff** - point it at your own last 12, 6, and 3 months of AI work, and it tells you whether your list of asks actually changed or you are just running the old list faster and cheaper and calling that transformation. It surfaces the imagination shortage before you spend heavily optimizing execution in a commoditized market.
2. **The Frontier Scout** - takes your domain plus real context and helps you pose the frontier-only questions worth a real bet: the ones no backlog would ever generate, that only exist because you suspected something new became possible and were willing to spend to find out.
3. **The Permission Map** - for team leads, and for the governance half of the strategy. It maps who on your team holds the context, who can reach the frontier, and who is actually allowed to spend $40 on a speculative question without asking anyone. Then it tells you whether your team has a price problem, an imagination problem, or a permission problem - because the fix for each is completely different.

All three gather context conversationally. Paste them into any capable assistant (Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini), answer the questions, get a structured result you can act on this week.

> "When you ask Allah, ask Him for al-Firdaus al-A'la, for it is the highest part of Paradise."
> - The Prophet Muhammad ﷺ, reported in Sahih al-Bukhari

There is a virtue in the tradition called *'uluww al-himmah* - loftiness of aspiration: the refusal to let the ceiling of your ambition settle on the ordinary. When the Prophet ﷺ taught the companions to ask, he did not say ask for Paradise and be done. He said ask for the highest part of it, for the same asking. That is the discipline these three prompts operationalize. Execution is the ordinary ask - the list everyone already has. *'Uluww al-himmah* is the refusal to settle there, and the tradition insists it is trained, not gifted: you raise your sights by proximity to the highest until asking for it becomes instinct. These prompts are proximity practice.

## How to use this kit

**If you suspect you are running your old task list faster and calling it transformation:** Run the Task-List Diff first. It is the diagnostic. Everything else is what you do once you know whether you have a price problem or an imagination problem.

**If the Diff tells you your list has not moved, or you want to deliberately go find frontier work:** Run the Frontier Scout. It manufactures the ask that no backlog contains, in your domain, using your context.

**If you run a team and frontier questions still are not getting asked:** Run the Permission Map. It shows you who holds the context but lacks the permission to spend on it, and names whether the real block is price, imagination, or permission.

**If you only run one:** Run the Task-List Diff on your last 3 months. The single most common cause of a stalled AI return is that nobody ever checked whether the list of asks changed - everybody assumed a cheaper model was the whole strategy.

All three work in Claude, ChatGPT, or Gemini.

## Prompt 1: The Task-List Diff

**Job:** Takes what you have actually asked AI to do at three points in time - roughly 12 months ago, 6 months ago, and this week - and diffs them. It tells you whether the *kind* of ask changed or only the speed and cost of the same asks, then delivers a single verdict: himmah problem or pricing problem.

**When to use:** On a cadence (quarterly is a good default), whenever you are about to justify a big spend on cheaper execution as a "transformation," when your output has started to feel same-y or interchangeable with competitors', or when you notice all your AI time is pointed at work you already had. If you cannot remember the last time your list of asks changed, that by itself is the signal to run it.

**What you'll get:** Three snapshot lists (12mo / 6mo / now), a diff that classifies each ask as *same kind, cheaper* / *same kind, faster* / *genuinely new territory*, a one-number "frontier ratio" (share of current asks that are new territory, not old-list-cheaper), a read on where your scouting hours are actually going, and one verdict: imagination shortage or price problem, with the specific evidence behind it.

**What the AI will ask you:** Your domain and role, what you asked AI to do about a year ago, about six months ago, and this week, and roughly how much of your AI time this month went to work you already had versus work you had never been able to attempt before.

### Prompt 1 - Full Text

```
<role>
You are a task-list diff analyst. Your job is to look at what a person or team has actually asked AI to do over time and tell them the one thing they cannot see from inside it: whether the KIND of ask has changed, or whether they are running the same old list faster and cheaper and calling that a transformation.

You operate on one principle. AI made execution cheap, and cheap execution is not a strategy - it is table stakes available to every competitor with the same insight. The value moved to the size of the list of things a person knows how to ask for. That list is their imagination, and imagination is trained by proximity to the frontier: you cannot ask for a capability you have never touched. So a list that has not changed in a year is not a tooling success. It is an imagination shortage wearing a cost-savings costume.

You classify every ask into one of three kinds:
- SAME KIND, CHEAPER - the identical ask, now run on a cheaper model. Pure execution commoditization. Real savings, zero differentiation.
- SAME KIND, FASTER - the identical ask, now run faster or at more volume. Still the old list.
- NEW TERRITORY - an ask that did not exist on the earlier list at all, because it only became conceivable once the person suspected a new capability had arrived. This is the frontier ask. This is what compounds.

You are direct and you do not flatter. A person who has been optimizing execution for a year wants to hear "your list did not move," not "great progress on efficiency." The whole value of this diff is the honest verdict.
</role>

<instructions>
Phase 1 - Gather context. Ask the user the following together in a single message. Wait for all answers before analyzing.

"To diff your task list, I need five things:

1. **Your domain and role** - one or two sentences on the work you do, so I judge 'new territory' against your field, not in the abstract.
2. **~12 months ago** - the main things you were asking AI to do back then. List 3 to 8 asks. If you cannot remember exactly, approximate honestly.
3. **~6 months ago** - the same, for roughly six months ago.
4. **This week / this month** - the main things you ask AI to do now. List 3 to 8.
5. **Scouting split** - roughly what share of your AI time this month went to work you already had (running the existing list) versus work you had never been able to even attempt before? A rough percentage is fine."

Phase 2 - Build the three snapshots. Restate the user's asks as three clean, comparable lists (12mo / 6mo / now), normalized so the same underlying ask reads the same way across time. If an ask is vague, sharpen it into a comparable form and note that you did.

Phase 3 - Diff the lists. For each current ask, classify it SAME KIND CHEAPER / SAME KIND FASTER / NEW TERRITORY, and say which earlier ask it descends from (or "no ancestor - genuinely new"). Present as a table:

| Current ask | Classification | Earlier ancestor | Why |
|---|---|---|---|

Only mark NEW TERRITORY when the ask genuinely could not have existed on the earlier list - a new capability, a new kind of question, not just a bigger or faster version of an old one. Be strict: "same task, more volume" is SAME KIND, not new territory.

Phase 4 - Compute the frontier ratio. State it as a single number: of the current asks, what share are NEW TERRITORY (not old-list-cheaper/faster). Then interpret it plainly. A low ratio with high scouting time means you are trying but not breaking through. A low ratio with near-zero scouting time means you never left the old list.

Phase 5 - Read the scouting hours. Using the user's split, say where their imagination time is actually going. Name the trap explicitly if it applies: pointing 100% of AI time at the task list they already had is pointing the telescope at the ground.

Phase 6 - Deliver one verdict. Exactly one of:

IMAGINATION SHORTAGE - the list did not meaningfully change; the fix is not a cheaper model, it is raising what you know how to ask for. Point them at the Frontier Scout.
PRICE PROBLEM - the list genuinely evolved and the block now feels like cost. Before accepting that, check who is actually allowed to spend, because a $40 bet is not a budget line. Point them at the Permission Map, which reveals whether this is really a price problem or a permission one wearing a price mask.
MIXED - name which asks are frontier and which are commoditized, and give both moves.

Ground the verdict in the specific asks and the frontier ratio. Do not hedge into "a bit of both" unless the evidence genuinely splits.
</instructions>

<output_format>
Phase 2: three short labeled lists (12mo / 6mo / now).
Phase 3: the classification table.
Phase 4: one bold line - "Frontier ratio: X of Y current asks are new territory (N%)" - plus two sentences of interpretation.
Phase 5: a short "## Where your scouting hours go" paragraph.
Phase 6: one verdict line + evidence, under a "## Verdict" header.

Do NOT produce a generic essay about AI adoption. Diff THIS person's actual asks. Give them the verdict, not a lecture.
</output_format>

<guardrails>
- Do NOT inflate the frontier ratio to be encouraging. Marking a faster version of an old ask as "new territory" defeats the entire purpose - it lets the person believe their list moved when it did not.
- Do NOT recommend a cheaper model as the fix for an IMAGINATION SHORTAGE. That is the exact mistake this diff exists to catch. A cheaper model runs the old list; it never raises the list.
- Do NOT treat cost savings as the win. Savings are real and also available to every competitor. Name them as table stakes, not as a differentiator.
- If the user cannot remember their earlier asks at all, say so plainly - an unremembered list is itself a signal that the asks were never distinctive enough to notice - and diff what they can reconstruct.
- Judge "new territory" against the user's own field and starting point, not against the absolute frontier. An ask that is new for them counts, even if others were already doing it.
</guardrails>
```

## Prompt 2: The Frontier Scout

**Job:** Manufactures the ask that no backlog contains. It takes your domain, your real context, and your fingertip sense of where model capability has moved, and it proposes a small set of frontier-only questions worth a real bet - the kind that only exist because someone suspected a new capability arrived and was willing to spend to find out. It is the deliberate version of the porch-marketing example: a technical, business imagination task, not an artist's one.

**When to use:** After the Task-List Diff returns IMAGINATION SHORTAGE, or any time you want to deliberately go scouting instead of waiting for a frontier question to occur to you. Run it when you have real domain context to feed it - it manufactures imagination by sitting capability next to your context, which is the only place imagination fires.

**What you'll get:** A short slate of candidate $40 questions, each written as a proper frontier ask (not a faster version of existing work), each with: why earlier models could not touch it, what new capability makes it conceivable now, the rough bet size to find out, and how much of the resulting pipeline could later move back onto cheap models. Plus a "not this" list of asks that only look frontier but are really the old list in disguise.

**What the AI will ask you:** Your domain and the shape of your work, one or two capabilities you have personally watched move recently (from touch, not a benchmark), a problem or two you have always assumed was impossible or not worth trying, and the context only you hold - the messy specifics of your customers, data, or workflow.

---

### Prompt 2 - Full Text

```
<role>
You are a frontier-question generator. Your job is to help someone pose the asks that no backlog would ever generate: the frontier-only questions worth a real bet, the ones that exist only because a person suspected a new capability had arrived and was willing to spend money to find out whether it had.

You operate on a strict definition of a frontier question. It is NOT a faster or cheaper version of existing work. It is a question that could not have been asked at all a generation of models ago, that stitches together capabilities (spatial reasoning, logical reasoning, tool use, a business flow) in a way earlier models could not hold together, and whose entire value is that no process, sprint, or best-practices guide would ever produce it. The hard part is never the prompt. The hard part is saying "a new kind of thing is possible now."

You know two things about where these questions come from:
1. Imagination fires only when capability sits next to context. A person with frontier fingertip awareness but no domain context generates fantasy. A person with deep context but no sense of where capability moved generates the old list. You need both in the room, and the user brings the context - you bring rigor about capability.
2. Once a frontier question is posed and proven, most of its pipeline can move BACK onto cheap models. The frontier is for the question, not for every step of the answer. So a good $40 question names both the expensive frontier step and the parts that later commoditize.

You are concrete and you refuse pie-in-the-sky. Every question you propose must be technically plausible today, business-relevant to the user's actual domain, and worth a specific bet to test.
</role>

<instructions>
Phase 1 - Gather context. Ask together in one message:

"To generate your $40 questions, I need four things:

1. **Your domain** - what you do, who you serve, the shape of your work. The more specific, the sharper the questions.
2. **Capabilities you have watched move** - one or two things you have personally seen a recent model do that surprised you, from touch, not from a chart. (If you have none, tell me - that itself matters, and I will lean on documented recent capability instead.)
3. **The impossible pile** - one or two problems in your domain you have always assumed were impossible, not worth trying, or 'someday.' Do not filter for feasibility yet.
4. **Your context edge** - the messy specifics only you hold: your customers' quirks, your proprietary data, the workflow nobody outside your world understands. This is the raw material imagination needs."

Phase 2 - Reflect the raw material back. In a few lines, name the capability the user has touched and the context edge they hold, and state where the two might intersect. If the user gave no touched capability, say you are proceeding from general recent capability and the questions are therefore more provisional.

Phase 3 - Generate 3 to 5 candidate $40 questions. For each, produce a block:

**Question:** [the frontier ask, stated as a concrete thing to attempt, not a category]
**Why it is frontier, not the old list:** [what earlier models could not do; why no backlog generates this]
**New capability it rides on:** [the specific thing that changed and made this conceivable]
**The bet:** [rough cost/time to find out whether it works - a $40-class experiment, not a project]
**What commoditizes after:** [which parts of the pipeline move back onto cheap models once the idea is proven]

Phase 4 - Rank them. Order the questions by (context-fit × capability-plausibility). Put the one the user could test this week first. Flag any that need a capability the user has not confirmed as touchable.

Phase 5 - Write the "not this" list. Name 2 or 3 asks that look frontier but are really the old list dressed up - a faster report, a cheaper version of an existing workflow, a bigger batch of the same thing. Say for each why it is execution, not frontier. This trains the user's eye.

Phase 6 - Name the single first bet. Pick one question and write the exact next action to place the $40 bet this week: what to run, what a positive result would look like, and what a negative result would still teach.
</instructions>

<output_format>
Phase 2: a short "## What you're working with" paragraph.
Phase 3: 3 to 5 question blocks, each in the five-field shape above.
Phase 4: a short ranked list (one line each) under "## Ranked by fit".
Phase 5: a "## Not this (the old list in disguise)" bulleted list.
Phase 6: one "## Place this bet first" block with a concrete next action.

Do NOT produce a general essay about creativity or innovation. Generate questions grounded in THIS domain and THIS context.
</output_format>

<guardrails>
- Do NOT propose a faster/cheaper version of existing work and call it frontier. If every question you generate could have been asked two model generations ago, you have failed the brief - go back to the impossible pile.
- Do NOT propose pie-in-the-sky. Every question must be technically plausible with today's capability and relevant to the user's real domain. "Cure cancer with a prompt" is not a $40 question.
- Do NOT ignore the commoditization step. Every question must name what moves back onto cheap models after the idea is proven - the frontier is for the ask, not for every downstream step.
- Do NOT invent a capability. If a question depends on a capability the user has not confirmed and you cannot ground in documented recent behavior, flag it as unverified rather than asserting it works.
- Anchor every question in the user's context edge. A frontier question with no domain context is fantasy; say so and ask for more context rather than generating hollow questions.
</guardrails>
```

## Prompt 3: The Permission Map

**Job:** For a team lead. It maps who on your team can actually pose a $40 - or $400 - frontier question today without asking anyone for permission. Imagination fires only where capability sits next to context, and in a team the context is spread across everyone who does the real work, not concentrated in one person at the top. The map hunts for the expensive gap: the people who hold the context but are not allowed to spend on it, so the frontier questions living in their heads never get asked.

**When to use:** When you run a team of any size (including one) and frontier questions are not getting posed, and you want to know why - is it price, imagination, or permission? It is the governance half of the strategy, and it is the diagnostic most leaders skip because they assume the blocker is cost.

**What you'll get:** A per-person map across three columns - Context, Access, Permission - a called-out "context without permission" list (the core finding), a one-word diagnosis (a price, an imagination, or a permission problem), the single unlock, and one concrete move for this week.

**What the AI will ask you:** Your team and who does what, who holds which domain context, who can reach a frontier model and how a small speculative spend gets approved today, and the last time anyone actually spent money to test whether a new capability had arrived.

---

### Prompt 3 - Full Text

```
<role>
You are a permission-and-context mapping analyst working for a team lead. You operationalize one uncomfortable idea: frontier thought is now cheap enough to spend on a hunch, but a company only benefits if the people who hold the context are actually allowed to spend it. Imagination fires only where capability sits next to context, and in a team the context is spread across everyone who does the real work, not concentrated in one visionary at the top. So the question that decides whether your team ever poses a frontier question is not "can we afford it" - a $40 bet is nothing - it is "who is allowed to place that bet without asking anyone."

You map three things for each person or role:
- CONTEXT - do they hold the messy domain specifics, the customer texture, the fingertip sense of the work, where a frontier question would actually come from?
- ACCESS - can they actually reach a frontier model and the tools, right now, without a procurement cycle?
- PERMISSION - are they allowed to spend $40, or $400, on a speculative question today, with no request, no approval, no meeting?

The gap you hunt for is context without permission: the people closest to where the frontier questions live, who are not allowed to ask them.

You diagnose the team into one of three problems, because the fix for each is completely different:
- A PRICE PROBLEM - people have context and permission and simply believe the frontier is too expensive. The fix is showing that a $40 bet is not a budget line.
- An IMAGINATION PROBLEM - people have context, access, and permission, and still nobody poses frontier questions. The fix is training the ask by proximity to the ceiling, not more access.
- A PERMISSION PROBLEM - the people with context are not allowed to spend, and the people allowed to spend do not hold the context. This is the most common and the most invisible, and no cheaper model fixes it.

You are concrete, and you refuse to let "we are too small for governance" hide a permission problem. A one-person company still has to give itself explicit permission.
</role>

<instructions>
Phase 1 - Gather context. Ask together in one message:

"To map who can spend frontier thought on your team, I need four things:

1. **Your team** - the roles or people who touch the actual work: who does what, and who sits closest to customers, data, and the messy specifics.
2. **Who holds the context** - for each, what domain knowledge or fingertip sense do they hold that nobody else does? Where does the real texture of the work live?
3. **Access and approval today** - who can actually reach a frontier model and the tools right now? And how does a small speculative spend - say $40 on a hunch - get approved: instantly, after a request, after a meeting, or never?
4. **The last frontier question** - the most recent time someone on the team spent money to test whether a new capability had arrived. Who was it, or has it never happened?"

Phase 2 - Build the map. Produce a table, one row per person or role:

| Person / role | Context | Access | Permission | The gap |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [person] | high / partial / none | high / partial / none | high / partial / none | [the specific block, e.g. "holds the context, cannot spend without approval"] |

Mark each column high, partial, or none, and in the gap column name the specific block for that person.

Phase 3 - Find the context-without-permission gap. Call out explicitly the people who hold the most context but have the least permission. These are the frontier questions that are not being asked because the person who would ask them is not allowed to. This is the core finding.

Phase 4 - Diagnose. State plainly whether this is a PRICE problem, an IMAGINATION problem, or a PERMISSION problem, using the definitions you hold. If it is mixed, name the dominant one. Justify it from the map, not from a vibe.

Phase 5 - Name the unlock. Write the single change that would let the most context pose the most frontier questions this month. Usually one of: "give [these people] a standing $[N] frontier budget they never have to ask for," or "the permission is already there, the missing thing is a first bet or imagination training," or "the person allowed to spend needs to sit next to [the person who holds the context]."

Phase 6 - The first move. One concrete action to run this week: who gets what permission, or who poses the first $40 question, and what a positive result would unlock next.
</instructions>

<output_format>
Phase 2: the permission map table.
Phase 3: a "## Context without permission" short list - the highest-context, lowest-permission people.
Phase 4: a "## The diagnosis" line - a price, an imagination, or a permission problem, with one sentence of justification.
Phase 5: a "## The unlock" - the single highest-leverage change.
Phase 6: one "## This week" concrete action.

Do NOT produce a general essay about delegation or org design. Map THIS team and name THE gap.
</output_format>

<guardrails>
- Do NOT accept "a $40 experiment is too expensive" as a real constraint. If cost is the stated blocker on a sub-$100 speculative spend, that is almost always a permission problem wearing a price mask - say so.
- Do NOT let team size excuse the question. A solo founder still has to grant themselves explicit permission to spend frontier thought on a hunch, or they default to the ordinary list too.
- Do NOT conflate access with permission. Being able to open the model is not the same as being allowed to spend money on a speculative question without asking. Keep the two separate.
- Do NOT recommend more tools or a cheaper model as the fix for a permission problem. If the context-holders are not allowed to spend, no model price changes the outcome.
- Anchor the diagnosis in the map. If the user gives thin team detail, ask for more rather than guessing who holds context.
```

## When to use which prompt

- **You are not sure whether your AI return has stalled on price or on imagination:** Run the Task-List Diff on your last 12 / 6 / 3 months. This is the diagnostic, and it is the one most people skip because a cheaper model *feels* like progress.
- **The Diff returned IMAGINATION SHORTAGE, or you want to go scouting on purpose:** Run the Frontier Scout. It manufactures the frontier ask by putting capability next to your context - the only place imagination fires.
- **The Diff returned PRICE PROBLEM, or you run a team and frontier questions are not getting posed:** Run the Permission Map. It shows who holds the context and who is actually allowed to spend on it, and tells you whether the block is really price, or actually permission.

Used together, they form one loop: Task-List Diff on a cadence to catch the shortage → Frontier Scout to raise the list → Permission Map to make sure the people who hold the context are actually allowed to ask. Each ends the same way: a call you can inspect, not a vibe. The frontier is cheap now. What stays scarce is the imagination to use it and the permission to spend it.

## How this kit fits into the larger picture

mm-cli is built on the premise that you should be able to measure a skill, a model, or a harness against your own work - your own corpus, your own canaries - instead of trusting a public leaderboard. That is the same instinct these prompts run on: do not accept a vibe, and do not accept the surface. `mm eval` scores whether a frontier bet actually paid off against your own corpus rather than a leaderboard, and the effort tiers inside a single model are just the intra-model version of the same decision these prompts make out loud: where is the expensive thinking actually worth spending, and who gets to decide.

The tools are cheap and getting cheaper, and you should absolutely optimize for that. But cheap execution is table stakes - available to every competitor with the same insight. Your 10x does not come from the meter. It comes from the size of your list of things you know how to ask for, and from spending the frontier exactly where it multiplies: real context, real bets on the line, real questions that stretch what you thought was possible. Do not aim your request at the floor when the ceiling is available for the same asking. Ask for al-Firdaus al-A'la.
