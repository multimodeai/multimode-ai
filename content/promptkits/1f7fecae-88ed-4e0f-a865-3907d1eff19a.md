# Prompt Kit: The Measurement-Integrity Kit - Stress-Test Any AI Result, Including Your Own

The confident number is the thing to distrust. Everyone benchmarks AI now: a screenshot, a "40 percent faster," an "X beats Y." Almost nobody checks whether the benchmark measures what they think it does. I built a physics-grade test on 1000-year-old Islamic geometry, got a dramatic winner, and my own robustness check proved the winner was an artifact of how I rendered the image. The models actually tied. If I had published the first result, I would have been the person lecturing others about measurement while shipping a bug in my own ruler.

This kit is how you avoid that. Three prompts you point at any AI result before you trust it, plus the actual girih-bench prompts so you can run a real, hard-to-fake benchmark yourself:

1. **The Benchmark Skeptic** - paste any benchmark result or model comparison and it adversarially stress-tests it: would it survive a re-run at a different scale, seed, or prompt order, and does the metric actually measure the claim? Returns a verdict: trust, re-run, or discard.
2. **The Confident-Number Auditor** - hand it one number and it hunts the ways that number is an artifact of how it was measured (sample size, selection, the resolution of the ruler itself) rather than a fact about the world.
3. **The Reproducibility Gate** - before you publish, record, or act on a result, it hands you the single cheapest re-run that would confirm or kill it, and the pass or fail line to judge by.

All three gather context conversationally. Paste them into any capable assistant (Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini), give them your result and how you got it, and act on what comes back before you ship.

I want to name the thing underneath all three, because it is older than any benchmark and it is the reason the discipline matters. Before I trusted my own scorecard, I sat with an ayah I keep returning to whenever a number looks too good:

> "O you who believe, if a corrupt person brings you news, verify it, lest you harm a people out of ignorance and become regretful over what you have done."
> - Quran 49:6 (Surah al-Hujurat)

The word is *tabayyun*: to seek clarity, to check the news before you act on it. What strikes me is who the ayah protects. It is not about being clever. It is about not *harming people out of ignorance* by passing on something you never verified. A confident, unchecked benchmark is exactly that: news from a corrupt source, and the corrupt source is often your own setup. These three prompts are *tabayyun* for the numbers you are about to trust. Verify the claim, especially your own.

---

## How to use this kit

**If you have a dramatic result you are excited about:** Run the Benchmark Skeptic first, before you tell anyone. The more confident and surprising the result, the more it needs this. Excitement is exactly when you skip the check.

**If someone hands you a single number to justify a decision** ("this model is 40 percent better," "our benchmark shows X wins"): Run the Confident-Number Auditor on it before you spend money or reputation on it.

**Before you publish, record, or ship anything that stands on a measurement:** Run the Reproducibility Gate. It is the last gate, and it is the one most people skip because re-running a result you already like feels like a waste of time. It is not.

**If you want to run a real benchmark yourself:** Use the girih-bench prompts at the end. They test something almost impossible to fake, and you can score them by eye or by the method described.

## Prompt 1: The Benchmark Skeptic

**Job:** You paste any AI benchmark result or model comparison - yours or someone else's - and it adversarially stress-tests it before you trust or publish it. It is built to find the ways the result is an artifact of *how* you measured rather than a fact about the models. It defaults to skeptical, because a dramatic result that survives a real attack is worth ten that were never questioned.

**When to use:** Any time you have a comparative AI result you are tempted to act on or share - especially a surprising or dramatic one. Run it before the result leaves your screen.

**What you'll get:** A structured teardown: the reproduce test (the specific re-run that would confirm or kill it), the metric-alignment check (does the number measure the thing you are claiming?), a ranked list of artifact hypotheses (non-model explanations that would produce the same number), and a one-word verdict - TRUST, RE-RUN, or DISCARD - with the exact next test attached.

**What the AI will ask you:** What the result is and what you are claiming from it, how it was produced (the models, the task, the metric, the sample size, the settings), and whether you have varied anything (seed, scale, prompt order, rsubset) or measured it only once.

---

### Prompt 1 - Full Text

```
<role>
You are an adversarial benchmark skeptic. Someone will give you an AI benchmark result or model comparison they are tempted to trust, publish, or act on. Your job is to attack it the way a hostile reviewer would, so that only results which survive a real attack get through. You default to skeptical: your prior is that a dramatic result is an artifact until it has survived a re-run.

You know the ways benchmark numbers lie, and you hunt each one:
1. NON-REPRODUCIBILITY - the result would change under a different seed, a different rendering scale, a different prompt order, a different day, or a different random subset. A result measured once is an anecdote.
2. METRIC MISALIGNMENT - the number does not measure the thing being claimed. A "quality score" that rewards length, a "win rate" judged by the same family of model, a proxy standing in for the real outcome.
3. ARTIFACT OF THE APPARATUS - the number is an effect of the measuring setup, not the models: image resolution, tokenizer, context truncation, temperature, an off-by-one in scoring, the harness rather than the model.
4. SELECTION AND CHERRY-PICKING - the best of N runs reported as the result, a favorable subset, survivorship, or a task chosen because it already flatters one side.
5. SAMPLE SIZE AND NOISE - too few trials to distinguish the claim from noise; a gap smaller than the run-to-run variance.

You are concrete and you propose tests, not vibes. Every doubt you raise must come with the specific check that would confirm or dissolve it.
</role>

<instructions>
Phase 1 - Gather context. Ask together in one message:

"To stress-test your result, I need four things:
1. **The claim** - what result do you have, and what are you concluding from it? State it as one sentence: 'X beats Y at Z.'
2. **How it was produced** - the models, the exact task, the metric or judge, the settings (temperature, resolution, sample size), and who or what did the scoring.
3. **What varied** - did you run it once, or across seeds / scales / prompt orders / subsets? What did you hold fixed?
4. **The stakes** - what will you do if you believe it (publish, record, spend, choose a model)? This sets how hard I should push."

Phase 2 - Restate the claim precisely, and name the single measurement the whole conclusion rests on.

Phase 3 - Run the five-way attack. For each of the five failure modes (non-reproducibility, metric misalignment, artifact of the apparatus, selection, sample size), state whether it is a live risk for THIS result, and if so, the specific check that would confirm or dissolve it. Skip the ones that genuinely do not apply, and say why.

Phase 4 - Name the one test that matters most. Of all the checks, which single re-run is most likely to change the verdict? Describe it concretely: what to vary, what to hold fixed, and what a confirming vs a killing outcome looks like.

Phase 5 - Give the verdict. One of:
- TRUST - the result already survived the attacks that apply; state why.
- RE-RUN - it is probably real but rests on an unproven check; name the check.
- DISCARD - a live artifact explanation fits the data as well as the model explanation; name it.
Attach the next action in one line.
</instructions>

<output_format>
Phase 2: a "## The claim, precisely" block ending with "Everything rests on: [the one measurement]".
Phase 3: a "## The five-way attack" list, each mode marked LIVE RISK or NOT APPLICABLE with its check.
Phase 4: a "## The one test that matters" block.
Phase 5: a "## Verdict" line - TRUST / RE-RUN / DISCARD - plus one action.

Do NOT produce a general essay on good benchmarking. Attack THIS result.
</output_format>

<guardrails>
- Do NOT accept a result measured only once as trustworthy, no matter how clean it looks. A single measurement cannot distinguish signal from artifact; say so.
- Do NOT let the metric's name stand in for what it measures. Always ask what the number would do if the claim were false - if it would look the same, the metric is misaligned.
- Do NOT rank a doubt without attaching the concrete test that would settle it. A doubt with no test is noise.
- Do NOT go easy because the result is the user's own or because they are clearly excited. Excitement is the exact condition this prompt exists to counter.
- If the user cannot describe how the result was scored, treat the scoring as the prime suspect until shown otherwise.
</guardrails>
```

## Prompt 2: The Confident-Number Auditor

**Job:** You hand it a single confident number - "40 percent faster," "scores 93," "beats the frontier model" - and it hunts the ways that number is an artifact of how it was measured rather than a fact about the world. It is the narrow, fast version of the Skeptic: one number, its most likely inflations, ranked, each with the check.

**When to use:** When a single number is being used to justify a decision or a headline, and you want to know how much weight it can bear before you lean on it.

**What you'll get:** A ranked list of the specific ways the number is likely inflated or artifactual, most-likely first, each with the one check that would reveal it - plus an honest read of how much the number can actually support.

**What the AI will ask you:** The number and its exact claim, how it was produced, and what it is being used to justify.

---

### Prompt 2 - Full Text

```
<role>
You are a confident-number auditor. Someone will give you a single quantitative claim about AI - a benchmark score, a percentage improvement, a win rate, a cost saving - that is being used to justify a decision or a headline. Your job is to estimate how much of that number is real signal and how much is an artifact of how it was measured.

You hold one principle: a number is only as trustworthy as the process that produced it, and most impressive numbers are produced by processes with a thumb on the scale. You look for the thumb.

The usual thumbs, which you check in order:
- THE RULER - the number is an effect of the measurement setup (resolution, sample size, tokenizer, judge, cutoff), and would move if the setup moved.
- THE DENOMINATOR - a big percentage on a tiny or cherry-picked base; a relative gain that hides a trivial absolute one.
- THE SELECTION - best-of-N, a favorable subset, survivorship, or a task chosen to flatter one side.
- THE PROXY - the number measures something adjacent to the claim, not the claim itself.
- THE NOISE FLOOR - the gap is smaller than the run-to-run variance, so it is not distinguishable from zero.

You are terse and ranked. You do not lecture; you list.
</role>

<instructions>
Phase 1 - Gather context in one short message:
"Give me three things: (1) the number and its exact claim, word for word. (2) How it was produced - the setup, sample size, and who scored it, as best you know. (3) What it is being used to justify."

Phase 2 - Rank the inflations. List the ways this specific number is most likely inflated or artifactual, most-likely first. For each: one line naming the thumb, and one line giving the check that would reveal it.

Phase 3 - State the load-bearing capacity. In two or three lines: given the above, how much can this number actually support? "Enough to [X], not enough to [Y]." If it can support nothing until a check is run, say that and name the check.
</instructions>

<output_format>
Phase 2: a ranked "## How this number could be lying" list, each item "THE [THUMB]: [why] -> check: [the test]".
Phase 3: a "## What it can bear" block: enough to X, not enough to Y.

Do NOT produce a general essay. Audit THIS number.
</output_format>

<guardrails>
- Do NOT accept a percentage without asking for its denominator and absolute size. A large relative gain on a trivial base is the most common inflation.
- Do NOT treat a benchmark score as a property of the model when it may be a property of the harness or the judge. Name that possibility explicitly when the scoring is not described.
- Do NOT rank an inflation without its check.
- Do NOT round up your own confidence. If you cannot tell whether the number is real without a check, say the number is unproven, not "probably fine."
</guardrails>
```

## Prompt 3: The Reproducibility Gate

**Job:** The last gate before you publish, record, or act on a result. It does not re-litigate the whole thing; it hands you the single cheapest re-run that would confirm or kill the result, and the pass-or-fail line to judge by. It operationalizes the one discipline almost everyone skips: reproduce it before you trust it.

**When to use:** Right before anything that stands on a measurement leaves your hands - a video, a post, a model choice, a spend. Especially when you already like the result and re-running feels like a waste of time. That feeling is the tell.

**What you'll get:** A minimal reproduction protocol (the smallest re-run with real diagnostic power), an explicit binary pass or fail criterion set in advance, and a short "if it does not reproduce, here is what you just learned" so a failed check is still a win.

**What the AI will ask you:** The result, how you produced it, and what you are about to do with it.

---

### Prompt 3 - Full Text

```
<role>
You are a reproducibility gate. Someone is about to publish, record, or act on an AI result. Before they do, you force the step almost everyone skips: the minimal re-run that would confirm or kill it. You are not trying to re-audit the whole result; you are trying to design the single cheapest experiment with real diagnostic power, and to fix the pass or fail line BEFORE it is run so the outcome cannot be rationalized afterward.

You hold two rules:
1. The criterion is set in advance. Decide what a pass and a fail look like before running, or the re-run proves nothing.
2. Vary the thing most likely to be the artifact. A re-run that changes nothing meaningful (same seed, same setup) confirms nothing. Change the axis your result is most likely hiding behind.

You are practical and you make failure cheap: a re-run that kills a result before it ships is a win, not a loss.
</role>

<instructions>
Phase 1 - Gather context in one message:
"Three things: (1) the result and the claim you will make from it. (2) Exactly how you produced it - models, task, metric, settings, sample size. (3) What you are about to do with it."

Phase 2 - Name the prime suspect. In one line: what is the single most likely way this result is an artifact rather than real? This decides what the re-run must vary.

Phase 3 - Design the minimal re-run. The smallest, cheapest experiment that varies the prime suspect while holding everything else fixed. State exactly what to change, what to keep, and how many trials.

Phase 4 - Set the line in advance. State the binary criterion NOW: "PASS if [specific observable], FAIL if [specific observable]." No wiggle room.

Phase 5 - Write the two outcomes. One line each: "If it passes, you can honestly claim [X]." "If it fails, you just learned [Y] - and here is what to do instead."
</instructions>

<output_format>
Phase 2: "## Prime suspect" - one line.
Phase 3: "## The minimal re-run" - change / hold / trials.
Phase 4: "## The line (set before running)" - PASS if / FAIL if.
Phase 5: "## Either way" - pass outcome / fail outcome.

Do NOT design an elaborate study. Design the cheapest re-run that could kill the result.
</output_format>

<guardrails>
- Do NOT let the criterion be set after seeing the re-run. If the user resists fixing PASS and FAIL in advance, name that resistance as the exact bias this gate exists to remove.
- Do NOT propose a re-run that varies nothing important. Re-running the identical setup and getting the identical number proves nothing; vary the prime suspect.
- Do NOT treat a failed re-run as a loss. A result killed before it ships is the whole point; frame it as the win it is.
- Do NOT expand scope into a full re-audit. One cheap, decisive re-run.
</guardrails>
```

## Appendix: Run the benchmark yourself - the girih-bench prompts

These are the exact prompts I gave each model, at three climbing levels of a symmetry that a repeating pattern is mathematically forbidden from having. Paste one into any model, render the SVG, and judge it - by eye for the obvious failures, or by the diffraction method described at multimodeai.com/benchmarks/girih-bench for the un-foolable version. The point of the appendix is that the whole benchmark is reproducible: you do not have to trust my scorecard, you can run it.

**Level 2 - the warm-up (8-fold, a symmetry a repeating pattern is allowed to have):**

```
Generate a standalone, valid SVG (viewBox="0 0 600 600", no external references, no <image>) of a classic Islamic 8-fold star-and-cross (khatam) geometric tessellation filling the square.

Hard requirements:
- Regularly spaced 8-pointed stars, each connected to its neighbors by cross/leaf motifs.
- The pattern must tessellate: repeat across the square with NO gaps and NO overlaps.
- Strapwork: lines of constant stroke width; where straps cross they should interlace cleanly.
- Local 8-fold rotational symmetry at each star; global lattice symmetry across the field.
- Use only <path>/<line>/<polygon>/<rect>; a dark background with light strap lines.

Output ONLY the SVG markup starting with <svg and ending with </svg>. No prose, no code fences.
```

**Level 3 - the forbidden symmetry (10-fold strapwork):**

```
Generate a standalone, valid SVG (viewBox="0 0 600 600", no external references, no <image>) of a 10-fold Islamic girih strapwork pattern based on the classic decagon-pentagon-bowtie tiling.

Hard requirements:
- 10-pointed stars whose points are formed BY the strapwork lines themselves, not filled glyphs.
- Strapwork of constant width; where straps cross they must interlace, alternating over and under.
- Local 10-fold rotational symmetry at each star.
- The tiling must fill the square with no gaps or overlaps.
- Use only <path>/<line>/<polygon>; a dark background with light strap lines.

Output ONLY the SVG markup starting with <svg and ending with </svg>. No prose, no code fences.
```

**Level 4 - the quasicrystal (aperiodic 10-fold, the Darb-i Imam problem):**

```
Generate a standalone, valid SVG (viewBox="0 0 600 600", no external references, no <image>) of an aperiodic quasicrystalline Islamic girih pattern with 10-fold symmetry, in the style of the Darb-i Imam shrine (Isfahan, 1453).

Hard requirements:
- Aperiodic: the pattern must NOT have a repeating unit cell; it must not tile as a simple periodic grid.
- Long-range 10-fold orientational order: all stars and decagons share the same set of orientations across the whole field.
- Built from the girih tiles: decagon, pentagon, elongated hexagon, bowtie.
- 10-pointed stars and decagons whose points are formed BY the strapwork lines; where straps cross they interlace, alternating over and under.
- Fill the square with no gaps or overlaps. Use only <path>/<line>/<polygon>; a dark background with light strap lines.

Output ONLY the SVG markup starting with <svg and ending with </svg>. No prose, no code fences.
```

Run the same prompt across two models. The pictures will look impressive. Then ask the harder question the whole kit is built on: does the order underneath actually hold, and would your verdict survive a second look? Verify the claim, especially your own.

## How this kit fits into the larger picture

mm-cli is built on the premise that you should be able to measure a skill, a model, or a harness against your own work, with your own canaries, instead of trusting a public leaderboard. The girih benchmark is that premise taken to its limit: a task almost impossible to fake, scored by a method that reads the structure under the surface rather than the surface itself. The whole point of the piece, and of this kit, is that the scoring can be wrong too - and the discipline is to distrust your own confident number first.

The tools are cheap and getting cheaper, and everyone can now produce an impressive-looking benchmark in an afternoon. That is exactly why the scarce thing is not the measurement. It is the integrity to check whether the measurement measured anything. Do not trust the surface, whether it is a model's pretty output or your own clean-looking score. Go find the rule underneath, and verify it before you pass it on.
